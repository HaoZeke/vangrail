* What Detects an Injection, and What It Costs

This gem detects injections with hand-written rules, which is one of four
families the published work uses and the weakest of them on every measurement
here. This page is the survey that says so, with the numbers measured on the
same corpus rather than quoted from each paper's own.

Every reference was checked before it was written down.

** Four families, and one that is not a detector at all

*** Hand-written rules

Patterns, lexicons, and templates: everything in =lib/vangrail/rails= except the
classifier. They cost microseconds, need no model, keep working when the
endpoint is down, and can be read and argued with by the person who has to
maintain them.

They also lose. On in-the-wild jailbreak prompts they catch 39.5% at a
false-alarm rate of 12.67%, and the false-alarm rate is not a dial: a rule stack
has exactly one operating point, and the only way to spend fewer false alarms is
to delete rules.

*** A bag of n-grams, fitted

The oldest working text classifier, and the cheapest thing that beats the rules.
Naive Bayes over word n-grams is the junk-mail recipe; a linear model fitted
discriminatively over hashed n-grams is the same features with the independence
assumption dropped, which is the =fastText= shape ([[https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1607.01759][10.48550/arXiv.1607.01759]]).

The difference between them is not academic here. Naive Bayes counts a word and
the pair containing it as two independent votes, so its score overstates itself
and has to be calibrated after the fact; fitting the weights jointly removes the
problem rather than correcting for it. Measured on the same folds: 67.0% for
naive Bayes, 73.7% for the linear model, both at the rails' 12.67%.

Neither needs a GPU, a network, or a dependency. The linear model is a flat
array of floats.

*** A fine-tuned encoder

The deployed shape of a published detector: a BERT-family encoder
([[https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.1810.04805][10.48550/arXiv.1810.04805]]),
usually DeBERTa ([[https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2006.03654][10.48550/arXiv.2006.03654]]),
fine-tuned on labelled injections. This is what ProtectAI's detector, Meta's
PromptGuard, InjecGuard ([[https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2410.22770][10.48550/arXiv.2410.22770]])
and PromptShield ([[https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2501.15145][10.48550/arXiv.2501.15145]])
all are.

It needs a model in the process or an endpoint in front of one, which is the
dependency this gem's whole design refuses. The measurement below is what it
buys.

*** The model as its own judge

Llama Guard ([[https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2312.06674][10.48550/arXiv.2312.06674]])
and the policy rails here: a generative model reading the text against a written
policy. Flexible, expensive per call, and reading an argument written to
persuade it when the text is a retrieved page.

A white-box variant reads the model's own attention rather than its output:
Attention Tracker ([[https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2411.00348][10.48550/arXiv.2411.00348]])
detects the distraction an injection causes, without training, and needs access
to the attention weights. DataSentinel
([[https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2504.11358][10.48550/arXiv.2504.11358]]) fine-tunes a
detector by a game between attacker and defender.

*** And the family that is not detection

StruQ ([[https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2402.06363][10.48550/arXiv.2402.06363]]),
SecAlign, Jatmo ([[https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2312.17673][10.48550/arXiv.2312.17673]])
and the instruction hierarchy
([[https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2404.13208][10.48550/arXiv.2404.13208]]) do not detect
anything. They change the model or the prompt so that the injection does not
work: separated channels, task-specific fine-tuning, a trained priority between
instruction sources.

They are the answer this repository cannot reach from outside the model, and
they are where the residual has to be paid for. A detector in front of a model
that will obey any instruction it reads is a filter in front of a hole.

** The finding two of those papers share with this repository

The surprise of the survey was not the ranking. It was that the false-alarm
problem this project measured by accident is the thing the recent detector
papers are about.

InjecGuard names it *over-defense*: guard models flag benign inputs because they
contain words that appear in attacks. Their benign evaluation set, NotInject, is
339 sentences enriched with trigger words, and they report state-of-the-art
models dropping to about 60% accuracy on it, which is close to guessing.

PromptShield's stated contribution is performance "in the low false positive
rate regime", on the argument that existing detectors are not deployable.

Both are the argument of [[file:evidence.org][the evidence page]] arrived at from
the other direction, and both are the finding of
[[file:external-evaluation.org][the external evaluation]] with a curated corpus
instead of a natural one. Where they build 339 benign sentences designed to
carry trigger words, this repository ran 18,258 manual pages that carry them
without being designed to:

| =Ignore all errors in commands executed to remake files.= | make's -i flag |
| =Print the authentication token gh uses for a hostname.= | a documented feature |
| =Enable debug mode.= | every tool with a debug flag |
| =331 Guest login ok, send your complete e-mail address as password.= | an FTP transcript |

The two corpora answer different questions. A curated one asks whether a
detector can be tricked by trigger words on purpose; a natural one asks how
often the trigger words occur anyway. A deployment needs the second number and
the literature mostly reports the first.

** Measured here, on one corpus, at one operating point

In-the-wild jailbreak prompts against the ordinary prompts collected beside
them, 1,405 against 13,735, everything cross-validated where it was fitted:

| Detector | Detection | False alarm |
|----------+-----------+-------------|
| hand-written rules | 39.5% | 12.67% |
| naive Bayes over n-grams | 67.0% | 12.66% |
| linear model over hashed n-grams | 73.7% | 12.67% |
| linear model, held to one false alarm in a hundred | 27.0% | 1.00% |

The last row is the one the rules cannot answer at all. A scored model has a
curve and a rule stack has a point, so only the model can be asked what it
catches when the desk will accept one false alarm in a hundred rather than one
in eight.

** What to use

For a documentation desk on a laptop, in this order.

Run the deterministic rails, because they cost nothing and they are the only
thing that works when the endpoint is down. Do not expect them to be the
defence: 39.5% of one corpus's jailbreaks, and 0% of the published indirect
injections, which is [[file:coverage.org][a gap no surface rule closes]].

Fit a linear model on your own traffic. It is thirty lines, it needs no
dependency, it beats the rules by thirty-four points on this corpus, and unlike
anything shipped it is fitted to the attacks you actually receive. The trainer
is =script/train_linear.rb=.

Put a fine-tuned encoder in front if you can afford the endpoint, and calibrate
it against your own benign corpus before believing its threshold, because
over-defense is what its own literature says it will do to you.

And spend the effort you have left on the training-time family instead, because
none of the above stops a model from obeying an instruction it reads. Detection
buys time and evidence; it does not buy correctness.
