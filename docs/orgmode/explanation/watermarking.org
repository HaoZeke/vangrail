* Marking Generated Text, and What the Mark Can Promise

Article 50(2) of the AI Act puts a duty on the provider of a generative system,
and it is worth reading in its own words:

#+BEGIN_QUOTE
Providers of AI systems, including general-purpose AI systems, generating
synthetic audio, image, video or text content, shall ensure that the outputs of
the AI system are marked in a machine-readable format and detectable as
artificially generated or manipulated.
#+END_QUOTE

The paragraph goes on to require that the technical solutions be effective,
interoperable, robust and reliable as far as this is technically feasible, taking
into account the specificities and limitations of content types, the costs of
implementation, and the generally acknowledged state of the art. It names no
technique. Recital 133 is where the examples live: "watermarks, metadata
identifications, cryptographic methods for proving provenance and authenticity of
content, logging methods, fingerprints or other techniques", to be applied at the
system or the model level.

A second duty sits in the second subparagraph of Article 50(4), on the deployer
rather than the provider:

#+BEGIN_QUOTE
Deployers of an AI system that generates or manipulates text which is published
with the purpose of informing the public on matters of public interest shall
disclose that the content has been artificially generated or manipulated.
#+END_QUOTE

That one is a sentence a reader can see, and it does not apply where the content
went through human review and a person or organisation holds editorial
responsibility for publishing it.

Two duties, two mechanisms. The visible sentence belongs in the application's
page and no library can write it for you. The machine-readable mark is a property
of the bytes, and that is =Vangrail::Watermark=.

** Why the sampler-side schemes are not available to an application

The watermarking literature is about token selection. Kirchenbauer et al.,
/A Watermark for Large Language Models/ (doi:10.48550/arXiv.2301.10226), split
the vocabulary with a keyed hash and tilt the logits toward one half, and a
key-holder measures the resulting skew. Dathathri et al., /Scalable watermarking
for identifying large language model outputs/, Nature 634, 818-823
(doi:10.1038/s41586-024-08025-4), replace the tilt with tournament sampling,
which preserves the model's distribution while leaving the same kind of
detectable trace. Christ, Gunn and Zamir, /Undetectable Watermarks for Language
Models/ (doi:10.48550/arXiv.2306.09194), give the cryptographic version, where
the sampling randomness itself comes from a pseudorandom function and the output
is computationally indistinguishable from unwatermarked text. Every one of them
is implemented by whoever runs the sampler.

An application calling an OpenAI-compatible endpoint does not sample. Under
bring-your-own-key it does not even know which sampler ran: the reader supplies
the endpoint, the model, and the key. There is no hook, and there is no artifact
in the returned text to measure, so a rail claiming to detect these on someone
else's output would be a rail reporting =certain?= falsely. If a provider ships a
detector endpoint, that is a networked rail against their API and it answers
about their models, not about ours.

What the application does own is the text after it arrives. So the mark goes
there, which has one property the sampler-side schemes cannot offer: it is
identical whichever model answered.

** Why the model is not asked to sign its own output

The obvious cheap version is a system-prompt instruction: end every answer with a
marker. It fails exactly where the obligation bites. A small model drops it. A
long context buries it. A reader asking for one line gets one line. A provider
trims trailing whitespace and the marker with it. Nothing about that is
reliable, and reliability is in the text of the article.

Post-processing is deterministic. The rail runs after the model, on every answer,
in the same code path for every endpoint.

** The format

Eleven bytes, appended to a paragraph's last character, one byte per Unicode
variation selector:

| Offset | Bytes | Meaning                                          |
|--------+-------+--------------------------------------------------|
|      0 |     2 | =0xA1 0x50=, fixed and public                    |
|      2 |     1 | format version, currently =1=                    |
|      3 |     8 | HMAC-SHA256 truncated to eight bytes, or all zero |

Byte values map to code points: =0x00= to =0x0F= as =U+FE00= to =U+FE0F=, and
=0x10= to =0xFF= as =U+E0100= to =U+E01EF=. Sixteen selectors in the BMP and 240
in the supplement is exactly 256, which is why the encoding is one byte per
selector and not something cleverer.

The HMAC covers the paragraph it is attached to, canonicalised: marks removed,
whitespace runs collapsed to single spaces, ends trimmed. The message is
=vangrail/watermark/v1=, the issuer string, and the canonical paragraph, joined
by newlines.

** What each half promises

The split between the magic bytes and the tag is the whole design.

Anybody holding the text can find =0xA1 0x50= and a version byte and know the
text was generated. No key, no agreement, no request to us: eleven lines of code
against the table above. That is the only reading of "interoperable" that means
anything, because a detector only we can run is not a disclosure. Fairoze et al.,
/Publicly-Detectable Watermarking for Language Models/
(doi:10.48550/arXiv.2310.18491), make the same split on the sampler side: they
sign the generation so that verification needs no secret, precisely because a
keyed-detector-only scheme cannot serve a public disclosure duty.

The issuer recomputes the HMAC and learns something stronger: this text is ours,
and the mark was not lifted off one answer and pasted onto other words. A mark
without the tag would be copyable, and a copyable mark is a disclosure rather
than an attribution. Both are useful and they are not the same claim, so they are
separate fields.

With no key configured the tag is eight zero bytes. The disclosure half still
works, which is the half the article requires, and a library that did nothing
until somebody provisioned a secret would ship turned off.

** Robustness, stated rather than implied

| Channel                                       | Mark survives |
|-----------------------------------------------+---------------|
| copy-paste between a browser and an editor    | yes           |
| a mail client that keeps Unicode              | yes           |
| a paragraph rewrapped or requoted             | yes           |
| one paragraph quoted out of six               | yes, on that paragraph |
| retyping, or a transcription                  | no            |
| an ASCII-only pipeline, or a format-character strip | no      |

The rewrap row is why the tag covers a canonicalised paragraph rather than the
whole answer: an answer-wide hash breaks the moment anybody quotes half of it,
and quoting half of it is what people do.

A format-character mark is weaker than a distributional one in one specific way
and it is worth naming rather than glossing. A green-list or tournament mark
degrades gracefully: Kirchenbauer et al., /On the Reliability of Watermarks for
Large Language Models/ (doi:10.48550/arXiv.2306.04634), measure survival under
human and machine paraphrase, and Zhao et al., /Provable Robust Watermarking for
AI-Generated Text/ (doi:10.48550/arXiv.2306.17439), give bounds under edit
distance. This mark does not degrade, it disappears: one pass through a tool that
normalises to ASCII removes it entirely, and there is no partial signal left to
measure.

What the distributional schemes buy with that robustness is a sampler hook we do
not have. What they do not buy is immunity: Krishna et al., /Paraphrasing evades
detectors of AI-generated text, but retrieval is an effective defense/
(doi:10.48550/arXiv.2303.13408), and Sadasivan et al., /Can AI-Generated Text be
Reliably Detected?/ (doi:10.48550/arXiv.2303.11156), both show a paraphrase pass
defeating detectors, and the second argues an accuracy ceiling as models approach
human text. No scheme in the literature survives a determined rewrite, so "as far
as technically feasible" is doing real work in the article, and the honest report
of that limit is this table rather than silence.

** Code is never marked

A variation selector inside a shell command is a command that fails, or worse,
one that runs and does something else. Fenced blocks and indented blocks come
back byte for byte, which costs a little coverage on an answer that is mostly
code and is not negotiable on a handbook whose readers copy commands into a
terminal.

** The two rails read the same carriers from opposite directions

=Rails::Obfuscation= strips invisible characters from questions and from
retrieved documents, and its =INVISIBLE= table plus =VARIATION_SELECTOR_RUN=
covers exactly the carrier this mark uses. That is not a conflict, it is the
order: the strip is =:input= and =:context=, the mark is =:output=. Text arriving
from outside loses its invisible characters; text leaving gains ours.

One consequence worth knowing: an answer pasted back into the same system as a
question arrives unmarked, because the inbound strip ran. That is the right trade
in both directions. Verification therefore happens at the boundary, before the
scrub, or on text that never went through it.

The carrier is the same one the attack literature is about. Boucher et al.,
/Bad Characters: Imperceptible NLP Attacks/ (doi:10.1109/SP46214.2022.9833641),
use invisible characters, homoglyphs, and reorderings to make text a model and a
reader disagree about, and Boucher and Anderson, /Trojan Source: Invisible
Vulnerabilities/ (doi:10.48550/arxiv.2111.00169), do the same to source code with
bidi controls. That is not an argument against the carrier, since there is nowhere
else in plain text to put a machine-readable mark, but it does set the
expectation: a pipeline hardened against those papers strips our mark too, and
should. A mark is a disclosure, not a defence, and it has no business surviving a
sanitiser.

** Where the mark does not go

Not in a header, not in a JSON field, not in an HTML attribute. Those are fine
and an application should have them too, and none of them survive a copy out of a
browser into a ticket, which is the journey the obligation is about. The mark
travels with the words or it does not travel.

** Who the obligation binds

The two duties land differently, and conflating them is how a compliance file ends
up asserting more than the code does.

Article 50(2) is about marking, it binds the provider of the generative system,
and it has no public-interest qualifier and no editorial-review exception. An
application built on somebody else's model is ordinarily the provider of its own
system and a deployer of the model underneath, so this is the duty that reaches a
desk running an assistant over its own documentation. It is also the one this
library implements.

The text half of Article 50(4) is narrower on both sides. It binds the deployer,
it covers text published to inform the public on matters of public interest, and
it lifts where a human reviewed the content and somebody holds editorial
responsibility for publishing it. Whether an answer shown to one reader who asked
a question is published to inform the public is a real question with a real answer
and this is not the document that settles it.

Which paragraph applies to a given deployment, and under which supervisory
authority, belongs to whoever owns the compliance file. What a library can do is
make the mark exist and publish the format, so that the determination is not also
an engineering project.

The legislation is Regulation (EU) 2024/1689,
[[http://data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj][data.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj]].
Article 50 carries the transparency obligations and Recitals 132 to 137 carry the
reasoning behind them.
