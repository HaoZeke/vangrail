* Blocked Is Not a Probability

Every rail in this gem answers a yes-or-no question, and =check_input= combines
those answers by taking the first yes. The prompt-injection literature does the
same: detectors are compared against each other, thresholds are tuned per
detector, and a stack of them is an OR gate.

That rule throws away almost everything the rails know. It cannot say how much a
hit is worth, cannot add up three near misses, cannot use the fact that a
sensitive rail stayed quiet, and hands an operator a word where a number was
needed. This page is about reading the same rails as evidence instead, which is
what =Engine#assess= does, and about the several things that only become sayable
once you do.

** What a hit is worth

The question a verdict should answer is not "did something match" but "how much
likelier is this text to be an attack now than it was before". That is a ratio,
and it is measurable: run the rail over attacks, run it over ordinary
documentation, and divide.

Measured over 270 attack texts and 48 benign ones, every rail against the same
handbook set (=script/measure_evidence.rb=, which writes
=tmp/handbook_evidence.rb=, not the shipped table):

| Rail                    | Caught  | Flagged | If it fires | Defensible at 95% | Capability at 1e-4 |
|-------------------------+---------+---------+-------------+-------------------+--------------------|
| =paraphrase=            | 203/270 | 0/48    | +6.2 bits   | +4.2 bits         | 0.282              |
| =injected_instructions= | 90/270  | 0/48    | +5.0 bits   | +2.9 bits         | 0.088              |
| =obfuscation=           | 60/270  | 0/48    | +4.5 bits   | +2.2 bits         | 0.049              |
| =similarity=            | 48/270  | 0/48    | +4.1 bits   | +1.9 bits         | 0.035              |
| =alignment=             | 47/270  | 0/48    | +4.1 bits   | +1.8 bits         | 0.034              |
| =hidden=                | 27/270  | 0/48    | +3.3 bits   | +0.9 bits         | 0.014              |
| =jailbreak=             | 16/270  | 0/48    | +2.6 bits   | -0.0 bits         | 0.006              |
| =many_shot=             | 6/270   | 0/48    | +1.2 bits   | -1.8 bits         | 0.001              |

A bit is a doubling of the odds. Silence is worth the other direction: a rail
that catches three quarters of everything and says nothing about this page has
told you something, and an OR gate cannot express it at all. =Engine#assess=
reads =EvidenceData=, written by =script/measure_evidence_external.rb= from
published attacks and real documents, not this handbook table.

The third column is the one to trust and the second is the one to be careful
with. A rate is not a number, it is a posterior over a number, and with a Beta
prior over a binomial count that posterior is a Beta whose pessimistic tail is
what a corpus of this size can defend. Read that way, =jailbreak= and
=many_shot= carry nothing: this corpus cannot show that a hit from either is
evidence of anything at all, and the point estimate was spending confidence
nobody measured. That is a fact about the corpus rather than about the rails,
and it is the single thing a larger benign set would change.
=Engine#assess(confidence: 0.95)= uses that bound for the posterior.
If the bound and the point estimate disagree about the action, the
judgement is uncertain: the action was not identified.

The origin of the span is a different question from what the rails
saw. A retrieved page that trips =paraphrase= is contaminated data,
not a user attack. =assess= labels the span (~input~ is user,
~context~ is data); =Session= keeps an attack track and a
contamination track, and they never add. =Conversation#screen= folds
pages onto contamination; =admit?= takes the last user turn as the
request. =Admission= refuses a capability whose request is not
privileged, or whose arguments are untrusted without an allowlist.
Detection still runs. Promotion is what it no longer does.
Measured on the shared 270/48 set at prior 1e-3: 270/270 attack
pages leave the attack posterior unchanged when labeled data;
267 move contamination; 267 would have moved the attack posterior
if labeled as the user. =admit?(:shell)= is false on all 318.

The last column is the information-theoretic reading of the same table: the
share of the uncertainty about "is this an attack" that a rail's verdict
actually removes at a given base rate, which is the measure the
intrusion-detection literature settled on for exactly this comparison. It is the
only number here that moves when the deployment does, and ranking rails by it
rather than by detection rate is what stops a rail that catches a sliver of the
corpus from looking useful.

** The base rate is the whole argument

Detector papers report their numbers on balanced corpora: half the traffic is an
attack. A documentation desk over an editable wiki might see one poisoned page in
ten thousand. Those two worlds disagree about what a hit means, and they
disagree by orders of magnitude.

| Base rate | Bits needed to reach even money |
|-----------+---------------------------------|
| 0.5       | 0.0                             |
| 1e-2      | 6.6                             |
| 1e-3      | 10.0                            |
| 1e-4      | 13.3                            |

No rail in the table above is worth half of 13.3 bits. At one attack in ten
thousand, the strongest rail here firing on a page takes it from one in ten
thousand to about one in a hundred and forty: a hundredfold increase, and still
almost certainly a false alarm.

That is not a criticism of these rails. It is what the arithmetic says about any
single detector, and it has a sharp consequence:

*** This is Axelsson's result, in a new domain

None of the above is new, and it is worth saying so plainly before the
arithmetic is used for anything. Intrusion detection worked through it
twenty-five years ago: Axelsson's base-rate fallacy paper is the same argument
against the same mistake, made when the detectors were network sensors and the
rare event was a network intrusion. Everything here that sounds like a finding
is that finding, restated for a documentation desk and a wiki page.

What this repository adds to it is not the argument but the measurement. The
rails are scored on one shared corpus, the operating points ship as a generated
table, and the arithmetic runs in the same process as the rails, so the base
rate stops being a remark in a discussion section and becomes an argument the
caller has to supply.

*** No one rail can justify a block, and no corpus can show otherwise

For a lone rail to carry a text from a base rate of 1e-4 to even money, its
false-alarm rate would have to be about 9e-5. Demonstrating a rate that low
needs roughly six thousand clean documents on which the rail stayed silent, and
that is with the most generous smoothing; a corpus of forty-eight bounds nothing
below about one in a hundred.

So a block that rests on one rail rests on a number nobody has measured, and the
honest way to reach a verdict at a realistic base rate is several rails agreeing.
Which is what =assess= reports, and what an OR gate cannot distinguish from one
rail firing.

#+begin_src ruby
judgement = engine.assess(page, side: :context, prior: 1e-4)
judgement.posterior   # => 0.74 for a page three rails object to
judgement.action      # => :block
judgement.fired       # => the rails, most telling first, in bits
#+end_src

The prior has no default and asking for one raises. Guessing on a deployment's
behalf would be the exact error this exists to expose.

** Three lines, not two

A posterior makes a middle action possible, and most of the interesting traffic
lands in it: one rail fired, the base rate is low, and the page is a hundred
times more suspicious than average and still probably fine. Blocking costs a
reader their answer. Ignoring it wastes the detection. Queueing it costs somebody
a minute.

=Policy= draws the two lines, and where they go is a deployment's judgement about
what a false block costs against what a missed injection costs. The library
ships a starting policy and says that is what it is.

** Where the two lines come from

A threshold with no cost behind it is a preference. The decision rule is the
ordinary one: allowing a page costs the chance it is an attack times what a
missed attack costs, blocking costs the chance it is fine times what a wrong
block costs a reader, and sending it to a person costs a minute of their time
whatever it turns out to be. Choosing the cheapest of the three gives both
lines directly.

#+begin_src ruby
Vangrail::Policy.from_costs(missed_attack: 1000, false_block: 10, review: 1)
# => block above 0.9, review above 0.001
#+end_src

Reviewing beats allowing above =review / missed_attack=; blocking beats
reviewing above =1 - review / false_block=. With no reviewer there is one line
and it is the classic =false_block / (false_block + missed_attack)=. A reviewer
who costs more than the mistakes collapses the band, and the library says so
rather than inverting the thresholds quietly.

The units cancel, so they can be euros or minutes. What they cannot be is
unstated: this is the arithmetic that turns a preference into a claim somebody
can argue with.

** Screening as ranking

=screen= drops a document the moment a rail objects. With a posterior there is
a better answer: rank the set, drop what the policy says to drop, queue what it
says to queue, and keep the rest with the doubtful pages last.

#+begin_src ruby
triage = engine.triage(documents, prior: 1e-4)
triage.kept      # documents, least suspicious last in the passage list
triage.review    # [{document:, judgement:}]
triage.dropped   # [{document:, judgement:}]
#+end_src

A page that trips one pattern at one attack in ten thousand is not a page worth
taking away from a reader. It is a page worth putting at the bottom.

** What only shows up in evidence

*** Silence counts

A clean page ends below its prior, because every rail that could have fired and
did not said something. Under an OR gate a clean page is indistinguishable from
a page nothing was run against.

*** An unread language is abstention

=Rails::Language= is posture, not a detector. It never blocks, and it
never contributes a term. When it reports that the page is outside the
lexicons, the word rails did not read the page, so their silence is not
recorded. The posterior stays at the prior and the judgement is
uncertain. Treating that silence as evidence of a clean page would be
the same error as treating a down endpoint as a pass.

*** Abstention is not innocence

A rail that was off, unreachable, or undecided contributes no term at all: a
likelihood ratio of one. This gem is unusual in knowing exactly which rails those
were, because =certain?= has always carried that fact, and here it finally has
arithmetic to feed. An endpoint that refused a connection must not read as
evidence of a clean page, and under a naive vote count it does.

*** Correlated rails vote once

Three rails firing on the same sentence for the same reason are one observation
reported three times, and summing them is how naive Bayes talks itself into
certainty. =script/measure_evidence.rb= measures the correlation on the handbook
set; the shipped table comes from =script/measure_evidence_external.rb=. Pairs
that agree are grouped, and a group contributes its single most informative
member.

On the shipped corpus no pair reaches the grouping threshold: the highest is
=alignment= with =similarity= at 0.49. The rails here really are
looking at different things, which is worth knowing and was not obvious. The
machinery stays because the next rail somebody adds may well duplicate one.

** Paying only for evidence that can change the answer

A rail's evidence is bounded by its measured operating point, so the interval the
unrun rails could still move the posterior into is a number. When the action is
the same at both ends of that interval, running them cannot change what happens.

#+begin_src ruby
engine.assess(page, side: :context, prior: 1e-4, escalate: true)
#+end_src

runs the free rails first and stops there when the answer is settled. On ordinary
traffic the deterministic rails put the result out of reach of anything the model
rails could say, so the round trip is never paid. The suite asserts over both
corpora at three base rates that the early stop never changes the action.

This is the part that pays for the framing rather than only tidying it. A desk
running a semantic rail pays for an embedding call on every document under the
switch; here it pays on the documents where the free evidence left the answer
open.

Skipping is not abstention, and they are kept apart. A rail proved irrelevant
leaves =certain?= true, because the claim is about the action and the action is
unchanged by construction. A rail that could not run makes the judgement
uncertain, because something that might have mattered did not happen.

** The sequence, and Wald

Sequential testing is older than any of this. Wald's ratio test accumulates the
log-likelihood ratio and stops when it crosses a threshold fixed by the two
error rates you are willing to accept, and network detection has used it in
exactly this shape: Jung's portscan work decides whether a remote host is
scanning by accumulating evidence across connection attempts, which is the same
problem as deciding whether a reader is probing across turns.

=Session= therefore reports both readings. The posterior answers "how likely is
this"; =verdict= answers "have I seen enough to decide, at error rates chosen in
advance", with thresholds =log((1 - beta) / alpha)= and =log(beta / (1 - alpha))=
rather than a number somebody liked. They disagree usefully: a session the test
calls undecided while the policy says review is one where the cost argument and
the error-rate argument point different ways, and somebody should know that.

** The sequence nobody checks

Staged probing is the attack family that gets past per-message detection: ask
something harmless, ask for more detail about the part of the answer that helped,
keep going. No message is an attack, so no per-message detector fires, and
=Rails::Escalation= only catches the crude version where a refusal is followed by
a retry.

Read as evidence it needs no new detector. Three turns that each move the odds by
two bits have moved them by six.

#+begin_src ruby
session = Vangrail::Session.new(engine: engine, prior: 1e-3)
session.observe(question)   # the turn's judgement
session.posterior           # the session's
#+end_src

Two properties keep it from becoming a session that eventually flags everyone.
Evidence decays between turns, so a session reflects recent behaviour rather than
everything since login, and ordinary turns contribute the silence of every rail
that ran, so a session recovers instead of only ratcheting.

Measured on the corpora: three probes that are each individually allowed take a
session to review; three ordinary turns take it back below where it started. And
because the decay is geometric, repeated probing converges on a ceiling of
per-turn bits divided by one minus the decay, rather than running away. An
operator can therefore say in advance how far a determined prober can push a
session, which is not a question a detector stack can be asked at all.

** Where this comes from

Almost every piece of machinery here is old, and the pieces that are not old are
small. Naming the sources is not decoration: it is the difference between a
design somebody can check against a literature and one that has to be taken on
trust.

- The base-rate argument is Axelsson's, for intrusion detection, in 2000.
- Combining detector outputs Bayesianly rather than by thresholds is Kruegel and
  colleagues, for intrusion detection, in 2003.
- The capability measure is Gu and colleagues, 2006, who make the same point
  about detection rates being the wrong summary when the event is rare.
- Sequential accumulation with thresholds from error rates is Wald, 1945, and
  the detection version is Jung and colleagues, 2004.
- Adding evidence from correlated sources without double counting is the
  subject of a whole literature on combining probability distributions; the
  grouping rule here is the crudest thing that respects it.
- Naive Bayes surviving violated independence has been studied directly, by
  Domingos and Pazzani and by Hand and Yu, and the answer is that the ordering
  of the classes survives much worse dependence than the probabilities do. This
  is why the posterior here should be read as a ranking with a scale attached
  rather than as a calibrated probability.
- Estimating a rate from few observations, and what to do about the events you
  have not seen, is Good's, and language modelling has a fifty-year literature
  on it that this borrows a single idea from.

What is left that this repository can claim is narrow: rails measured on one
shared corpus so their evidence is commensurable, abstention wired to the
=certain?= flag that the rails already carried, an early stop proved against the
bound rather than a confidence heuristic, and the whole thing shipped as a
generated table beside the rails it describes. Whether any of that is new I
cannot say from one search; what I can say is that the sources above are where
it came from, and that none of the prompt-injection work cited in the README
does it.

** What this does not fix

*** The numbers are this corpus

The attack half was written by the same hand as the rails, and it will be
optimistic about attacks that hand did not think of. What the table is for is the
ratio between rails, measured on the same texts; read as absolute detection rates
they claim far more than they know.

*** The false-alarm rates are bounds, not measurements

No rail fired on any of the 48 benign texts, so no false-alarm rate in the table
is a measurement. The Beta bound is the honest reading and it is why two rails
fall to zero evidence at 95% confidence. A benign corpus in the thousands is the
single biggest improvement available to this table, and it costs nothing but
ordinary documentation.

*** The threat mix is a modelling choice

Every number moves if the corpus does. A rail measured against a corpus
containing none of the attacks it was built for scores zero catches and the
arithmetic concludes a hit from it is evidence of innocence, which is exactly
what happened here before the encoded and pasted families were added. A
deployment whose traffic looks different should rerun
=script/measure_evidence_external.rb= rather than inherit these numbers.

*** The posterior is a ranking with a scale, not a calibrated probability

Naive Bayes is famously good at ordering and famously bad at the numbers, and
nothing here changes that. A judgement of 0.74 means "well above the ones that
scored 0.003", not "74 of every 100 pages like this are attacks". Calibrating it
properly needs held-out data with labels, which is a thing a deployment can
build and this repository cannot ship.

*** Independence is handled, not solved

Grouping stops the measured duplicates from double counting. It does nothing
about the subtler dependence between rails that share a corpus, a language, or an
author's blind spot, and there is no correlation matrix that would.

*** The prior is unknown to the library and knowable by the deployment

An application can estimate it: sample retrieved pages, have somebody read them,
count. That is tedious and it is the single number with the most influence over
every verdict here, which is a reasonable trade for a day's work and an unusual
thing for a guardrail to ask for.
